\section{Introduction}\label{sec:introduction}

Governments increasingly invest in place-based resilience---flood defenses, drainage, hardened transport links, and other infrastructure that lowers the probability that production is disrupted by climate-related hazards. A standard evaluation asks how much safer the protected location becomes. For firms, however, resilience is an organizational choice as well as a property of public infrastructure. They can locate primary production in different places and maintain alternative facilities or contingency capacity elsewhere. Public protection therefore changes both physical risk and private production architecture.

This paper asks whether decentralized competition for mobile production can make those two margins conflict. Suppose a local government improves climate protection. The direct effect is beneficial: primary production in that jurisdiction becomes less likely to fail. The jurisdiction also becomes more attractive to firms. But a safer primary location lowers the private return to costly geographic backup capacity. If firms reduce backup readiness, the national production system may become more dependent on the protected place. A coordinated planner internalizes that response. A local government additionally values attracting primary production from the other jurisdiction.

The main result is that these forces can produce a qualitative policy conflict. There exists a nonempty open set of primitives for which the coordinated planner chooses zero additional local climate protection while decentralized local governments choose a strictly positive symmetric protection level. The result does not arise because protection directly destroys surplus. Holding private redundancy fixed, protection has a positive engineering benefit. The social marginal effect becomes negative only after firms endogenously reduce geographic backup readiness. Yet the local marginal incentive remains positive because protection attracts mobile primary production.

The model has two jurisdictions and two differentiated-product firms. Local governments first choose public protection. Firms then choose primary production locations and costly backup readiness in another location. Localized disasters occur, after which the available firms compete in quantities. When both firms survive they earn duopoly profits; when only one survives it receives a scarcity rent. This difference makes backup choices strategic substitutes and links product-market rivalry to private resilience. More substitutable products increase the profit difference between sole-survivor and duopoly states and reduce equilibrium backup readiness in the frozen model.

The mechanism is easiest to understand through its nested benchmarks. If primary locations are fixed, public protection can still crowd out private backup capacity, but a local government cannot use protection to attract production. If private redundancy is fixed, the model reduces toward familiar competition for mobile firms through local public inputs: decentralized governments may overprovide the place-based input, but public policy cannot reorganize private resilience. In the full model, the two responses interact. Private redundancy adjustment can make additional common protection socially undesirable, while the plant-attraction motive causes local governments to provide it anyway.

This distinction matters because each component has important prior art. Regional governments have long been modeled as competing for mobile oligopolistic firms through local public inputs \citep{WalzWellisch1996,MaurerWalz2000}, and mobility can change the direction of public-input distortions \citep{Matsumoto1998,Ihara2008}. Environmental policy can also change plant locations and market structure \citep{MarkusenMoreyOlewiler1993,MarkusenMoreyOlewiler1995}. Separately, private adaptation and resilience can be inefficient under market power or other externalities \citep{Eisenack2014,CapponiDuStiglitz2024}, firms diversify sourcing locations in response to climate risk \citep{CastroVincenziEtAl2024}, and recent evidence indicates that public flood protection can crowd out private defensive investment \citep{ZhaoYangZhang2026}. Accordingly, this paper does not claim novelty for plant-attraction overinvestment, adaptation crowd-out, or private resilience underinvestment. The contribution is the full-game policy ranking that is absent from either benchmark alone.

The institutional interpretation is deliberately narrower than a general assessment of climate-adaptation spending. Real protection projects generate benefits to households, lives, public assets, and sectors outside the model. The result instead identifies an industrial-resilience margin that can be missed when project appraisal treats private production organization as fixed. Japanese policy documents provide direct examples of both decentralized climate-adaptation planning and firms' use of alternative facilities and sites as business-continuity measures \citep{MOE2026,SMEA_BCP}. Industrial policy also explicitly involves local efforts to attract production and develop suitable industrial sites \citep{METI2026,EHIME2026}.
