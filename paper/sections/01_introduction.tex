\section{Introduction}\label{sec:introduction}

Place-based climate adaptation changes both physical risk and firms' organizational responses to that risk. Flood defenses, drainage, and hardened local infrastructure can make primary production more reliable. Firms can also preserve production through geographically separate contingency arrangements. A protection project's industrial benefit therefore depends on how firms adjust backup readiness and where they place primary establishments, not only on its engineering effect on failure probabilities.

This paper asks whether competition for mobile production can sustain local protection after its national industrial marginal value has become negative. A safer primary site reduces the private return to maintaining backup readiness. That substitution need not be harmful: it saves real preparedness costs. The relevant distortion is that firms do not generally capture the full social value of production availability. When the lost surplus from induced backup reductions exceeds both those cost savings and the direct reliability gain, additional public protection lowers industrial welfare. A local government can nevertheless gain because protection attracts primary production and its location-attached benefits from the other jurisdiction.

The main result establishes that this possibility survives the complete policy game. Within the maintained symmetric primitive family, a nonempty open set has a unique zero optimum for a coordinated protection-policy planner and a positive symmetric decentralized Nash equilibrium. The planner coordinates protection while retaining private location, backup, and product-market choices. Its industrial-surplus objective includes consumer and producer surplus and resilience costs. Benefits to lives, housing, and other assets lie outside this comparison, so its policy ranking applies to the industrial component of adaptation appraisal.

The model has two jurisdictions and two differentiated-product firms. Governments choose local protection, firms privately observe location-fit shifters and choose primary sites, and then choose costly contingency readiness before localized disasters and Cournot competition. Co-location makes primary failures correlated. A sole survivor earns more than a firm facing an active rival, so backup choices are strategic substitutes when products interact. More substitutable products reduce symmetric equilibrium readiness on the maintained interior branch.

The contribution concerns a protective-policy instrument linking two established lines of theory. \citet{GrossmanHelpmanLhuillier2023} analyze geographic backup sourcing, production availability, consumer-surplus and business-stealing externalities, and policies toward diversification and reshoring. \citet{WalzWellisch1996} and \citet{MaurerWalz2000} study public-input competition for mobile oligopolistic production. Here the competing input reduces primary disruption risk and changes the private substitute for that input. The resulting public-protection margin can be negative even though its direct engineering effect is positive, while plant attraction maintains a positive decentralized incentive. Exact whole-domain arguments establish this joint policy ranking, including all unilateral policy deviations.

Three analytical comparisons identify the mechanism. First, at private backup choices, an explicit social--private readiness wedge explains why crowd-out can reduce welfare after preparedness-cost savings are included. Second, at the symmetric origin the local marginal payoff satisfies $M_L=M_S/4+2b\lambda_0$, where $\lambda_0$ measures plant attraction. The decomposition follows from the incidence rule; its content is the endogenous possibility $M_S<0$ together with $\lambda_0>0$ and sufficiently valuable plant attraction. Third, an exact benchmark with zero incremental sole-survivor rent generates the same global ranking. Product-availability surplus that firms do not capture is sufficient for this example; oligopoly adds readiness responses to correlated failures and product substitutability.

The closest environmental comparisons already establish important components. \citet{KouskyLuttmerZeckhauser2006} show that government disaster protection and private investment in risk-prone locations are jointly determined and that better protection can attract private capital; they explicitly set aside complementarity or substitutability between public and private protection. \citet{GramesEtAl2019} analyze a firm's joint flood-risk location and defense choices, while \citet{MahmudBarbier2016} model how public storm-protection programs affect private self-protection and self-insurance. The safe-development literature examines protection-induced exposure and weaker preparedness \citep{HaerEtAl2020,FusinatoEtAl2024}. Zhao, Yang, and Zhang (2026, unpublished working paper) report firm-level public-protection crowd-out. \citet{Eisenack2014} studies private adaptation with endogenous market structure. \citet{MartinHerranEtAl2026} analyze strategic regional environmental policy and industrial allocation in a transboundary-pollution game. Section~\ref{sec:literature} compares these overlaps and the distinct policy question.

For adaptation appraisal, the result separates a direct reliability benefit, an induced private-resilience response, and a jurisdictional attraction motive. A comparison between engineering benefits and construction cost can miss the private--social readiness wedge. Coordination of the protective input alone leaves that underlying distortion in place. The theorem supplies an existence result; the frequency and magnitude of the conflict remain empirical questions.
