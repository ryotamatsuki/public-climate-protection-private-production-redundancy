\section{Introduction}\label{sec:introduction}

Climate-adaptation policy changes more than physical exposure to hazards. Governments increasingly invest in place-based resilience---flood defenses, drainage, hardened transport links, and other infrastructure that lowers the probability that production is disrupted by climate-related hazards. Firms, however, also choose how resilient their production systems are. They can locate primary production in different places and maintain alternative facilities or contingency capacity elsewhere. Public protection can therefore alter private adaptation itself. Evaluating a protection project while holding firms' production architecture fixed can miss an endogenous response that matters for welfare.

This paper asks whether decentralized competition for mobile production can make public and private resilience margins conflict. Suppose a local government improves climate protection. The direct effect is beneficial: primary production in that jurisdiction becomes less likely to fail. The jurisdiction also becomes more attractive to firms. But a safer primary location lowers the private return to costly geographic backup capacity. If firms reduce backup readiness, the national production system may become more dependent on the protected place. A coordinated planner internalizes that response. A local government additionally values attracting primary production from the other jurisdiction.

The main result is that these forces can produce a qualitative policy conflict. Within the maintained symmetric primitive family, there exists a nonempty open set for which the coordinated planner chooses zero additional local climate protection while decentralized local governments choose a strictly positive symmetric protection level. The result does not arise because protection directly destroys surplus. Holding private redundancy fixed, protection has a positive engineering benefit. The social marginal effect becomes negative only after firms endogenously reduce geographic backup readiness. Yet the local marginal incentive remains positive because protection attracts mobile primary production. A general marginal decomposition makes this wedge transparent: at the symmetric origin, the local marginal incentive equals one quarter of the national marginal value plus the plant-attraction term $2b\lambda_0$, where $\lambda_0$ is the response of the jurisdiction's plant-location probability to its own protection. Thus the local incentive can remain positive after the national marginal value turns negative whenever the attraction term is sufficiently strong.

The model has two jurisdictions and two differentiated-product firms. Local governments first choose public protection. Firms then choose primary production locations and costly backup readiness in another location before localized disasters occur and surviving firms compete in quantities. When both firms survive they earn duopoly profits; when only one survives it receives a scarcity rent. This difference makes backup choices strategic substitutes and links product-market rivalry to private resilience. On the maintained symmetric interior branch, more substitutable products increase the profit difference between sole-survivor and duopoly states and reduce equilibrium backup readiness. This level effect does not imply that substitutability strengthens the marginal readiness response to public protection; Section~\ref{sec:institutions} states the interaction prediction separately.

The mechanism is easiest to understand through its nested benchmarks. If primary locations are fixed, public protection can still crowd out private backup capacity, but a local government cannot use protection to attract production. If private redundancy is fixed, the model reduces toward familiar competition for mobile firms through local public inputs: decentralized governments may overprovide the place-based input, but public policy cannot reorganize private resilience. In the full model, the two responses interact. Private redundancy adjustment can make additional common protection socially undesirable, while the plant-attraction motive causes local governments to provide it anyway.

The paper connects three literatures that have largely studied separate margins. Regional governments have long been modeled as competing for mobile oligopolistic firms through local public inputs \citep{WalzWellisch1996,MaurerWalz2000}, and environmental policy can change plant locations and market structure \citep{MarkusenMoreyOlewiler1993,MarkusenMoreyOlewiler1995}. Most closely on environmental policy and industrial allocation, \citet{MartinHerranEtAl2026} study strategic regional environmental policy and the long-run spatial distribution of industry in a transboundary-pollution dynamic game. The present mechanism is different: the policy variable is protection against disruption, and its welfare effect operates partly through firms' endogenous geographic backup capacity and the resulting disaster-state market structure. In a flood-risk setting, \citet{GramesEtAl2019} jointly study a firm's location and private flood-defense choices and show that existing flood defense can induce location closer to water and lower private defense investment. A 2026 working paper by Zhao, Yang, and Zhang (SSRN 6996377) likewise reports evidence that public flood protection can crowd out private defensive investment. These papers establish that public protection can reshape private adaptation and location; this paper asks what follows when that response occurs inside a mobile oligopoly and local governments compete to attract production. Related work also links local-government competition to industrial resilience through market segmentation and industrial-structure distortions \citep{MengDingWang2024}, studies inefficient private adaptation under endogenous market structure \citep{Eisenack2014}, analyzes resilience under market power and network externalities \citep{CapponiDuStiglitz2024}, and documents geographic diversification in response to climate risk \citep{CastroVincenziEtAl2024}. Accordingly, the contribution is not any one of these mechanisms in isolation. It is the policy-ranking reversal created when protection-induced geographic-redundancy adjustment interacts with decentralized competition for mobile oligopolistic production.

For environmental policy, the result identifies a specific appraisal margin. Real climate-protection projects generate benefits to households, lives, public assets, and sectors outside the model, so the theorem is not a general argument against adaptation spending. Rather, it shows that the industrial component of project benefits cannot in general be evaluated by treating firms' resilience choices as fixed. Public protection can substitute for private geographic redundancy, while decentralized jurisdictions may still have an incentive to expand protection because the same policy attracts mobile production. The model therefore separates a direct engineering benefit, an endogenous private-adaptation response, and an interjurisdictional attraction motive.
