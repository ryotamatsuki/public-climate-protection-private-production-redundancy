\section{Related Literature}\label{sec:literature}

Two bodies of theory are especially close. \citet{GrossmanHelpmanLhuillier2023} study supply disruptions and geographic backup sourcing. They identify consumer-surplus, business-stealing, and markup distortions in private resilience choices and distinguish first-best instruments from constrained diversification and sourcing policies. These insights absorb any broad novelty claim for geographic redundancy, state-dependent market power, or a social--private resilience wedge. Our planner is likewise constrained, but the instrument is a public reduction in primary-site risk rather than a diversification or sourcing subsidy. Two local governments choose that protective input to attract mobile primary establishments. The new policy question is whether this endogenous substitute for private readiness can have negative national industrial value while attracting positive decentralized provision. Proposition~\ref{prop:zero-delta} makes clear that incremental disaster-state monopoly rents are not necessary for this result.

The other close comparison is regional competition for local public inputs. \citet{WalzWellisch1996} analyze strategic provision with endogenous oligopolistic location; \citet{MaurerWalz2000} study two governments competing for two mobile oligopolistic firms. They already establish the strategic architecture and the possibility of inefficient infrastructure provision. Mobility and market integration can change the direction of local-public-good distortions \citep{Matsumoto1998,Ihara2008}. Here the public input's national marginal product is itself altered by private contingency investment. Proposition~\ref{prop:attraction-threshold} is an incidence-based decomposition, not an independently novel accounting identity. Theorem~\ref{thm:main} establishes that a negative common-policy value caused by endogenous readiness can coexist with positive, globally incentive-compatible local protection and unique continuations. This is a specific adaptation-policy implication of the two established lines of theory, not a claim that combining familiar ingredients by itself establishes novelty.

Public protection, private defense, and location have also been studied together. \citet{GramesEtAl2019} show in a firm's flood-risk investment/location problem that existing defense can encourage location closer to water and reduce private defense. The safe-development literature studies analogous induced exposure and preparedness responses \citep{HaerEtAl2020,FusinatoEtAl2024}. \citet{ZhaoYangZhang2026}, a working paper, report public flood-protection crowd-out of firms' defensive investment and increased tail-risk losses. \citet{GawelEtAl2018} discuss public-choice barriers and public/private substitution in climate adaptation. These studies absorb the broad protection-induced crowd-out and location mechanism. Our result concerns policy coordination versus a simultaneous local-government game after the private production continuation is re-solved.

Environmental policy can change industrial allocation and market structure \citep{MarkusenMoreyOlewiler1993,MarkusenMoreyOlewiler1995}. Closest within ERE, \citet{MartinHerranEtAl2026} analyze industrial allocation under regional transboundary-pollution policy. Their policy addresses pollution and operates through a dynamic spatial industry game. We analyze disruption protection and its substitute, private contingency readiness, in a static game. \citet{OtazawaEtAl2016} study industrial agglomeration and natural-hazard vulnerability. \citet{BacchiocchiEtAl2024} jointly study location, supply-chain disruption, and government subsidies in a dynamic game. Neither the presence of firm location nor government involvement in resilience is new here; the distinctive comparison is between coordinated and competing protective risk reduction with endogenous backup choice.

Finally, \citet{Eisenack2014} studies inefficient private adaptation with endogenous market structure; \citet{CapponiDuStiglitz2024} analyze supply-network resilience under market power and incompleteness; and \citet{CastroVincenziEtAl2024} examine geographic sourcing diversification under climate risk. \citet{GnutzmannEtAl2020} link capacity-based syndicate incentives to spare capacity and disaster resilience, showing why a general assertion that more competition always increases resilience would be inappropriate. \citet{MengDingWang2024} connect local-government competition and industrial resilience through market segmentation and industrial-structure distortions. Our restricted readiness comparative static and exact protective-policy ranking should be read alongside these different market and institutional mechanisms.
