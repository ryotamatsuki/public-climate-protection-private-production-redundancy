\section{Related Literature}\label{sec:literature}

Two bodies of theory are especially close. \citet{GrossmanHelpmanLhuillier2023} study supply disruptions and geographic backup sourcing. They identify consumer-surplus, business-stealing, and markup distortions in private resilience choices and distinguish first-best instruments from constrained diversification and sourcing policies. These mechanisms underpin the private-readiness distortion studied here. Our planner is likewise constrained, but the instrument is a public reduction in primary-site risk. Two local governments choose that protective input to attract mobile primary establishments. The policy question is whether this endogenous substitute for private readiness can have negative national industrial value while attracting positive decentralized provision. Proposition~\ref{prop:zero-delta} shows that incremental disaster-state monopoly rents are not necessary for this result.

The other close comparison is regional competition for local public inputs. \citet{WalzWellisch1996} analyze strategic provision with endogenous oligopolistic location; \citet{MaurerWalz2000} study two governments competing for two mobile oligopolistic firms. They establish the strategic architecture and the possibility of inefficient infrastructure provision. Mobility and market integration can change the direction of local-public-good distortions \citep{Matsumoto1998,Ihara2008}. Here private contingency investment alters the public input's national marginal product. Proposition~\ref{prop:attraction-threshold} decomposes that value under the maintained incidence rule. Theorem~\ref{thm:main} establishes that a negative common-policy value caused by endogenous readiness coexists with positive local protection, global best responses, and unique continuations. Its contribution is this specific adaptation-policy implication within the stated model family.

Public protection, private defense, and location have also been studied together. \citet{KouskyLuttmerZeckhauser2006} are a particularly close precursor: government protection and private investment in risky locations are jointly determined, greater protection attracts private capital, and the feedback can generate non-concave benefits and multiple equilibria. They explicitly set aside complementarity or substitutability between public and private protective investment. \citet{GramesEtAl2019} show in a firm's flood-risk investment/location problem that existing defense can encourage location closer to water and reduce private defense. \citet{MahmudBarbier2016} model how public storm-protection programs and natural barriers affect private self-protection and self-insurance. The safe-development literature studies analogous induced exposure and preparedness responses \citep{HaerEtAl2020,FusinatoEtAl2024}. Zhao, Yang, and Zhang's unpublished working paper \emph{Trading Resilience for Growth: Evidence from Public Flood Protection in China} (2026, \href{https://doi.org/10.2139/ssrn.6996377}{SSRN 6996377}) reports crowd-out of firms' defensive investment and increased tail-risk losses. \citet{GawelEtAl2018} discuss public-choice barriers and public/private substitution in climate adaptation. These studies establish the broader investment and protection feedbacks. Our result concerns the policy ranking in a simultaneous local-government competition game after mobile oligopolistic production and private geographic backup are jointly re-solved.

Environmental policy can change industrial allocation and market structure \citep{MarkusenMoreyOlewiler1993,MarkusenMoreyOlewiler1995}. Closest within ERE, \citet{MartinHerranEtAl2026} analyze industrial allocation under regional transboundary-pollution policy. Their policy addresses pollution and operates through a dynamic spatial industry game. We analyze disruption protection and its substitute, private contingency readiness, in a static game. \citet{OtazawaEtAl2016} study industrial agglomeration and natural-hazard vulnerability. \citet{BacchiocchiEtAl2024} jointly study location, supply-chain disruption, and government subsidies in a dynamic game. Neither the presence of firm location nor government involvement in resilience is new here; the distinctive comparison is between coordinated and competing protective risk reduction with endogenous backup choice.

Public/private protection interaction also appears in prevention models outside environmental economics. \citet{LeePinto2009} study multi-jurisdictional prevention with spatial responses, and \citet{HickeyEtAl2021} distinguish technological from equilibrium substitution and complementarity between public policing and private protection. \citet{FrieheMungan2022} show that political choice can overprovide public policing when public and private protection are substitutes. In a duopoly model of digital piracy, \citet{ChoiBaeJun2010} show that stronger public protection can reduce private protection and increase piracy when private choices are strategic substitutes. Thus both substitution and distorted public provision have precedents; the local incentive here is attraction of primary production, and private readiness affects product-availability surplus. Within urban adaptation, \citet{HirteEtAl2018} show that distributional incentives and an emphasis on output rather than welfare can lead to excessive protective investment. Our comparison instead holds the industrial-surplus incidence fixed and identifies the role of firms' contingency choices.

Finally, \citet{Eisenack2014} studies inefficient private adaptation with endogenous market structure. The unpublished studies \emph{Are Supply Networks Efficiently Resilient?} by Capponi, Du, and Stiglitz (2024, \href{https://doi.org/10.17016/FEDS.2024.031}{FEDS 2024-031}) and \emph{Weathering the Storm: Supply Chains and Climate Risk} by Castro-Vincenzi, Khanna, Morales, and Pandalai-Nayar (2024, \href{https://doi.org/10.3386/w32218}{NBER Working Paper 32218}) examine network resilience under market power and incompleteness, and geographic sourcing under climate risk, respectively. \citet{GnutzmannEtAl2020} link capacity-based syndicate incentives to spare capacity and disaster resilience. \citet{MengDingWang2024} connect local-government competition and industrial resilience through market segmentation and industrial-structure distortions. These different mechanisms delimit the interpretation of our restricted readiness comparative static and protective-policy ranking.
