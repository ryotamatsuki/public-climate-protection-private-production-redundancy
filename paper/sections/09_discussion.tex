\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Policy and welfare scope}

The model isolates industrial surplus under a restricted protection-policy menu. It excludes protection benefits to lives, households, public infrastructure, other assets, and sectors outside the two-firm market. It also leaves private backup and Cournot distortions in place. Consequently, the zero coordinated optimum is not a first-best recommendation against adaptation. The policy implication is to evaluate local protection together with induced contingency-readiness and location responses, and to distinguish coordination of public spending from correction of private resilience incentives.

The dependence on omitted direct benefits can be made explicit. If the planner also receives $\mu(a_A+a_B)$, then its common-policy origin marginal becomes $M_S+2\mu$. At the canonical witness, $\mu>0.00324123212$ makes that marginal positive, so zero is no longer its optimum. This is a local sensitivity calculation, not an estimate of real project benefits or a solution of the augmented policy game. It states precisely why the theorem must be read as an industrial-component ranking.

The government objective is also conditional on equal, policy-independent shares of market surplus and a state-independent hosting benefit. Location-dependent ownership, local consumer exposure, employment that depends on post-disaster operation, or a tax system financing protection could change the policy wedge. These are substantive microfoundations, not alternative labels for the present objective.

\subsection{Risk and readiness primitives}

The baseline imposes perfect primary-failure correlation within a jurisdiction, independence across jurisdictions, and conditionally independent contingency success. Readiness gives access to the unconstrained Cournot output at the same production cost. Backup sites, common backup hazards, capacity limits, and state-dependent operating costs are not chosen. These assumptions make the correlated availability margin explicit, but restrict portability. Multi-site backup, correlated regional disasters, taxes, and alternative product-market competition would require a new continuation and welfare analysis. The exact theorem does not cover them.

\subsection{Diagnostic portability}

The global theorem is an existence result in the maintained symmetric baseline family, supported by strict whole-domain margins and a simple policy root. It is not an all-parameter or all-microfoundation claim. The marginal identity $M_L=M_S/4+2b\lambda_0$ extends to any symmetric origin with the same incidence, hosting benefit, and zero public marginal cost, but global planner optimality and a Nash equilibrium require additional conditions.

Two specified numerical diagnostics probe functional forms. Replacing linear risk by $q(a)=q_0\exp(-a/q_0)$ produces a positive symmetric candidate near $0.01041$, with no profitable deviation found by the stated full-interval numerical search, and a planner maximum at zero on the tested grid and multistart searches. The nonlinear specification matches both $q(0)$ and $q'(0)$, so equality of origin marginals is built in; the changed positive-policy candidate and off-origin checks provide the additional diagnostic content.

Replacing the uniform fit distribution by a centered logistic distribution with scale $H/2$ yields the baseline symmetric candidate near $0.02302$ and passes the specified numerical continuation and deviation searches. The symmetric agreement is also structural. If a choice CDF has $L(0)=1/2$ and $L'(0)=1/(2H)$, differentiating its fixed-point equation at a symmetric policy gives the same $p_{a_A}$ as the uniform law. Expected surplus is stationary in $p$ there, so the entire symmetric government FOC is identical. Matching the logistic density therefore forces the same symmetric stationary candidate; it is not independent evidence of a generally robust policy ranking. The informative checks are its off-diagonal location continuation and deviations against that candidate.

For each tested policy pair, the logistic contraction bound establishes uniqueness of its location continuation. A finite policy grid does not establish that bound on the entire policy square. Neither diagnostic proves global planner signs or all unilateral incentives for an alternative family. The numerical evidence and its grids, tolerances, and optimization procedures are reported separately from the baseline certificates.

The same adaptation question may arise for data centers or logistics operators with alternative production or routing arrangements, but a corresponding application would have to justify the risk, readiness, and government-incidence assumptions. The remaining limitations include those microfoundations and policy instruments, as well as the empirical frequency and magnitude of the conflict. They are not merely a missing calibration.
