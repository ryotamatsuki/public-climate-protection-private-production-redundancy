Dear Editors of Environmental and Resource Economics,

Please consider the manuscript “Public Climate Protection and Private Production Redundancy: Local Resilience Competition, Plant Location, and Disaster-State Market Power” for publication in Environmental and Resource Economics.

The paper studies a climate-adaptation problem in which public protection and firms’ private resilience choices are jointly endogenous. Two jurisdictions invest in local protection, mobile oligopolistic firms choose primary production locations and geographic backup readiness, localized disasters occur, and surviving firms compete in the product market. The central result is a Protection–Attraction Conflict: within a nonempty open set in the maintained symmetric primitive family, coordinated welfare is maximized by zero additional local protection while decentralized jurisdictions choose strictly positive protection. The conflict arises because public protection has a positive direct engineering benefit but crowds out private geographic redundancy, while local governments retain an additional incentive to attract mobile production.

The paper contributes to environmental economics in three ways. First, it treats private production redundancy as an endogenous adaptation margin rather than a fixed background condition in public-protection appraisal. Second, it connects this adaptation response to industrial location and decentralized environmental policy competition. Third, it derives a transparent marginal decomposition that separates the direct protection benefit, the induced private-resilience response, and the plant-attraction motive. The global equilibrium result is supported by exact computer-assisted certificates and a complete replication package. Pre-specified diagnostic re-solutions under a nonlinear protection technology and logistic location heterogeneity preserve the canonical policy-ranking conflict, while the exact theorem remains tied to the maintained baseline family.

The paper is distinct from work on environmental policy and industrial allocation, including Martín-Herrán, Martínez-García, and Morales (2026), because the policy instrument here is protection against disruption and the key welfare channel runs through firms’ endogenous geographic backup capacity and disaster-state market structure. It is also distinct from work showing that public flood protection affects private adaptation or location because those mechanisms are embedded here in a mobile oligopoly with strategic local-government competition, generating a policy-ranking reversal.

The manuscript is original, has not been published elsewhere, and is not under consideration by another journal. The anonymous manuscript and an anonymized replication archive are prepared for double-anonymous review.
